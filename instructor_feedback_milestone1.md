# Instructor Feedback on Milestone 1

## Collective Feedback on Topic, Scholarly Context, and Collaboration Plan

I really like your shared focus on how money moves through college sports. Your individual projects connect well without all asking the same question: revenue, spending, athlete valuation, and payments between schools give you different perspectives on the same system. I also appreciated that you defined what falls outside the scope. That should help keep the collective website focused rather than becoming a general account of college athletics.

I think your recognition that similar-looking numbers do not necessarily measure the same thing is particularly useful for this course. The shared question is not only how much money there is, but how institutions define, classify, estimate, and make that money visible. Who produces a figure, what purpose does it serve, and what becomes harder to see when it is presented as a single number? Those questions could connect the individual projects and help you develop your collective principles.

Your scholarly context is a strong start because you explain how the research relates to particular projects. Reporting, commercial websites, and data documentation are useful evidence too, but they have different roles from published scholarship.

The main piece to clarify is the distinction between custom creation and auditing. Collecting or merging existing figures can involve meaningful computation, but I also need to understand what close research and interpretation each of you will contribute. You might create contextual records about definitions, reporting decisions, or relationships that the existing databases do not preserve.

I think your collaboration plan sounds feasible, and I appreciate the emphasis on reviewing one another's work. Your next step is to implement the individual folders and shared website structure, with a main README that helps users navigate the data, code, and documentation. Please use group logs and commits to make everyone's contributions visible, including work outside GitHub. You could also develop shared definitions for terms such as revenue, spending, valuation, payment, and earnings, so readers understand why they should not be treated as interchangeable. 

Overall, I think this is a strong start to a project that could show how financial relationships in college sports are constructed, represented, and made visible. I look forward to seeing how you develop your custom datasets and audits in the next milestone!

### Brayden

I like your interest in comparing financial reporting across schools and the conference itself. EADA, Knight-Newhouse, and the Big Ten's Form 990 give you identifiable sources to investigate, and your recognition that they do not all measure the same thing is a useful starting point for an audit.

I think your next step though is to narrow the years, institutions, and categories. A school's athletic department and a conference are different reporting levels, so their totals should not automatically become equivalent observations. What will one row in your custom dataset represent: an institution and year, a financial category, or a decision about how two records can be compared?

One approach might be to create 50-100 records documenting how particular categories line up, or fail to line up, across sources. You could preserve the original label, definition, reporting level, evidence for your interpretation, and uncertainty. That would make the intellectual labor of comparison visible rather than simply combine the figures into a table that appears naturally consistent. Cases where you decide not to make a comparison could be valuable records too.

Computation could help you extract tables from PDFs where accessible, match institution names, check dates, or identify missing values. You could also build a small tool to assist with recording your category decisions. If you standardize labels, retain the original wording so future users can see what changed and why.

I also like your idea for the audit, and particularly think investigating where each source's records originate, what its reporting definitions are, and how it handles missing information or different reporting periods is strong. One thing you'll need to do is consider how you'll verify selected figures against the underlying sources rather than assume agreement proves accuracy. Finally, the links you included seemed to be primarily to potential sources, but I noted that you listed the Lawrence, Gabriel and Tuttle as a potential source. That seems very relevant, and you might also bring in some of our readings around the history of quantification and missing data. Happy to help brainstorm further leads as you develop the focus further, but generally think this is very strong and particularly you might take a look at how ProPublica and Knight-Newhouse document their sources and definitions. Excited to see how this develops as you go and please reach out if you have any questions!

### Chenglin

I really like your idea of examining guarantee games, especially because it asks what an ordinary game record leaves out about the financial relationship behind a matchup. CollegeFootballData can provide the visible sporting event, while contracts and reporting from sources such as FBSchedules and *Front Office Sports* may reveal the payment that helped produce it. That gives you a particularly clear connection between a custom-created dataset and an audit of existing data.

I think your next step though is to narrow the seasons, institutions, or types of matchups and explain how you will select approximately 50-100 games. One row could represent a single game, but you should define what qualifies as a guarantee game and what evidence is sufficient for inclusion. You might focus on one or two seasons, a particular conference, or games between FBS and FCS schools. A bounded selection will make it easier to research each payment carefully rather than assemble a much larger list with uneven evidence.

For the custom dataset, I would suggest preserving the source of each payout and distinguishing a contractual amount, an amount reported by a journalist, and evidence of an actual payment where available. Those may not be the same thing, and an unavailable figure should remain missing rather than become a zero. I would also encourage you to capture more than payout and score where the sources support it. Location, institutional level, cancellation terms, contract date, and notes about the relationship between the schools could help explain what the transaction represents. Missingness and uncertainty could become meaningful parts of the dataset rather than problems to hide.

You'll also need to work in computation. Code could help you collect accessible game records from CollegeFootballData, extract candidate text or tables from contracts, standardize team names, and match dates across sources. You will still need to verify extracted figures against the original documents and explain why a particular contract or report matches a game. A script can connect records, but it cannot decide on its own whether payments reported under different agreements have the same meaning.

For the audit, I think you should investigate CollegeFootballData's fields, sources, coverage, and documentation rather than only note that it lacks payout information. Which version of the game does the database make available, and what changes when your financial records are placed alongside it? You might verify selected game records and document any inconsistencies you find. The Ballou and Nordstrom scholarship you identified seems like a useful foundation for thinking about athlete risk and the larger institutional relationship behind these games. A payout or score difference alone would not establish harm, so their work can help you decide what other context and evidence your dataset should preserve. Overall, I think this could become a very strong project about the financial structure hidden behind an apparently ordinary game record. Excited to see how it develops, and please reach out if you want help narrowing the sample!
